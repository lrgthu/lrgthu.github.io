*Forthcoming research retrospective*

Years ago, I worked on learning a transformation of visual features from neural similarity so that the transformed space better matched brain-response geometry and improved encoding prediction. At the time, that felt like strong evidence that the learned representation was more brain-aligned.

I now ask a harder set of questions.

> **What alternative explanation did the gain defeat?**

Would the same improvement appear with shuffled neural targets? With matched non-neural supervision? With a more flexible readout? Does the effect survive a held-out participant, a new stimulus distribution, or an intervention that distinguishes competing mechanisms?

The change is not from believing prediction matters to believing it does not. It is a change in what I think prediction licenses us to say.

The trajectory is roughly:

`better neural prediction`

→ `specificity against matched controls`

→ `model or mechanism identification`

→ `prospective falsification`

The finished note will use my own earlier contrastive brain-alignment project and the later residual-supervision falsification study as bookends. The point is not to rewrite the older work with hindsight, but to make visible how a researcher's evidential standard changes as the questions become sharper.
